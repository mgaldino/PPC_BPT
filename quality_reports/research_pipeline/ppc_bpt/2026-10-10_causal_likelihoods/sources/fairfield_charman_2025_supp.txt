Appendix A: Additional Considerations Regarding Hypotheses and Priors



1. Hypothesis Articulation



Readers might wonder if a Bayesian approach can be applied in contexts where rival hypotheses
are not as well developed as in our example applications in Sections 3 and 5. If the alternative
hypotheses remain somewhat vague, we can often still proceed in a Bayesian manner, at least
during early stages of research. For instance, if we start with hypotheses that lack detailed
causal mechanisms, it may still be possible to evaluate relative likelihoods for some of the
evidence that turns up. John Snow’s investigation of cholera is an example. Snow lived well
before the germ theory of disease, but he was able to find evidence that weighed strongly in
favor of waterborne contagion vs. airborne contagion (e.g., information about the distribution
of cholera deaths around the Broad Street pump), even without any detailed understanding of
how exposure to contaminated water or to bad air might produce disease in the human body.

In social science contexts, however, we do need to aim for well specified rival hypotheses. If a
hypotheses is left too vague, it can be difficult to figure out what kinds of observations would
be expected and what kinds of observations would be surprising, which poses problems for
reasoning about relative likelihoods. Within a Bayesian framework, articulating hypotheses is
often an iterative process—after collecting data, we may need to go back and flesh out the
hypotheses so that they make clearer predictions with respect to particular kinds of evidence
that have turned up. Likewise, It is reasonable to begin a research project with vague or very
tentative ideas about possible explanations, but moving forward, we must refine our ideas,
figure out our argument, and compare it to rivals. It is the Bayesian scholar’s job not only to
articulate their own argument, but also to articulate rivals for comparison.

2. Relative Import of Priors



Although Bayesianism is strongly associated with priors, this is arguably the least important
part of Bayesian analysis when it comes to model comparison. The fundamental difference be-
tween frequentism (the more dominant inferential framework in social science) and Bayesianism
lies in the definition of probability itself (Jaynes 2003)—for logical Bayesians, probabilities are
rational degrees of belief, and the concept of probability can be applied to any proposition,
including hypotheses and nonstochastic data, whereas frequentism understands probability in
terms of repeated random experiments and restricts the applicability of the concept exclusively
to stochastic data.

While the “Statistics Wars” between Frequentists and Bayesians have often focused on questions
of prior probabilities, Bayesians like Harold Jeffreys have emphasized the use of Bayes factors,
and statisticians like I.J. Good have emphasized the conceptual utility of their logarithms
(weights of evidence) much more than posterior probabilities themselves, precisely because the
former separate the import of the evidence from prior beliefs about the theories.

As per Eq. 2 (main text), both prior log-odds and weights of evidence contribute additively to
posterior log-odds. Ideally, we would like to be in a situation where the weight of evidence over-
whelms our prior log-odds. Priors are the most subjective component of Bayesian analysis—it
is difficult to directly evaluate the relative plausibility of hypotheses in light of large amounts
of background knowledge. Evaluating the likelihoods of concrete empirical observations condi-
tional on a hypothesis is an easier endeavor that can be conducted much more systematically.
Moreover, when the weight of evidence is very strong, scholars who begin with different prior
log-odds may all end up with posterior log-odds that favor the same hypothesis. That is, strong
evidence can bring scholars with substantial initial disagreements to a state of posterior agree-
ment. Of course, we do not always end up with strong evidence that overwhelms our priors.
We are currently working on another project that illustrates the opposite extreme, where the
prior log-odds are very strong and end up overwhelming the weight of evidence.

3. Additional Hypotheses for the Big Oil Example



Two additional hypotheses beyond strategic accommodation and competitive advantage are
plausible alternatives that could also be considered:

Leveling (HL )
Since 2015, oil majors have endorsed moderate climate policies first and foremost when they
perceive that those policies will level the playing field with competitors. (E.g., Falkner 2008) In
the absence of such perceived benefits, they are unlikely to support climate policies. 1

Costless Reputational Gains (HR )
Since 2015, oil majors have endorsed moderate climate policies first and foremost when they
perceive them as politically infeasible, in order to take advantage of cost-free opportunities for
improving their reputation among climate-conscious citizens and policymakers. Reputational
gains can in turn attract green-minded consumers, preclude boycotts, and/or help keep other
politically feasible climate policies from gaining salience.

The leveling hypothesis is similar to the competitive advantage hypothesis, but it postulates
a distinct rationale and distinct conditions under which we should anticipate support from
Big Oil. Under HL , oil majors support moderate climate policies that eliminate disadvantages
they are presently facing with respect to competitors, whereas under HCA , they seek to gain
new competitive advantages to exploit at their competitors’ expense. Likewise, the reputation
hypothesis shares a broadly similar rationale with strategic accommodation, in that reputational
gains may help to reduce or preclude demand from society for more stringent regulations.
But these hypotheses make distinct predictions about the kinds of climate policies that oil
majors support. Under HR , they advocate infeasible policies that pose no real threat, whereas
we interpret HSA as implying support for policies that oil majors envision could actually be
adopted, for the sake of locking in the lesser evil and closing down political space for more
costly alternatives. We would make this implication more explicit in the wording of HCA for
clarity if we were including HR in our analysis.

From a Bayesian perspective, introducing additional hypotheses (or revising an existing hy-
pothesis) poses no particular difficulties beyond some additional inferential work. The set of
hypotheses needs to be examined carefully and reformulated as needed to make sure that the al-
ternatives are indeed mutually exclusive rivals. One then proceeds with pairwise comparisons—
we must analyze prior odds involving the new hypotheses as well as likelihood ratios involving
the new hypotheses for each piece of evidence. For the Big Oil example, we leave this to future
work.

To give an example where logical dependence is relatively easy to handle, our analysis in Section
5 did in fact involve conditioning on previously analyzed evidence—most importantly, knowing
E0 (information pertaining to the independent variables invoked by each hypotheses) mattered
for our reasoning about subsequent evidence regarding the oil companies’ policy positions (the

given the companies’ shifts toward natural gas production (E0e ), the competitive-advantage

Big Oil example, we (implicitly) indicated logical dependence in our reasoning by noting in
parentheses the previously introduced evidence upon which we conditioned.

2. Details Matter for Inferential Weight



We mentioned in Section 4 that even seemingly small details in the evidence can matter for
relative likelihoods and inferential weight. We provide a brief example here to illustrate, drawing
on evidence from the Big Oil application. In Section 5, we argued that the following evidence
very weakly favors HCA (W oE ≈ 3 dB):

E2 = In 2017 while President Trump was moving toward withdrawing from the Paris Agreement,
US oil majors Exxon and Chevron publicly urged him to reconsider. In a widely reported letter to
Trump, Exxon’s CEO argued that the US was “well positioned to compete within the framework
of the Paris Agreement with abundant low-carbon resources such as natural gas,” and that
remaining in the accord would allow the US to retain “a seat at the negotiating table to ensure
a level playing field so that all energy sources and technologies are treated equitably in an open,
transparent and competitive global market.”

We then noted that Vormedal et al. imply that the letter also mentioned that “Paris could
provide a boost for natural gas.” We could find no record of that detail, but let us consider
hypothetical evidence E20 that includes it:

E20 = In 2017 while President Trump was moving toward withdrawing from the Paris Agreement,
US oil majors Exxon and Chevron publicly urged him to reconsider. In a widely reported letter to
Trump, Exxon’s CEO argued that the US was “well positioned to compete within the framework
of the Paris Agreement with abundant low-carbon resources such as natural gas,” explicitly
asserted that “Paris could provide a boost for natural gas,” and added that remaining
in the accord would allow the US to retain “a seat at the negotiating table to ensure a level playing
field so that all energy sources and technologies are treated equitably in an open, transparent
and competitive global market.”

Thanks to these additional details, E20 weighs more strongly in favor of competitive advantage
(HCA ). While our analysis of the elements common to E2 and E20 remains unchanged, seeing
Exxon make an explicit link between the Paris agreement and increased gas sales would be
much more expected under the competitive advantage hypothesis as compared to the hedging
(strategic accommodation) hypothesis, where concerns over precluding more radical climate
policies should predominate.

Now consider another variation. Suppose that Exxon’s letter had not included any mention
of being “well positioned to compete” within the Paris framework (and no explicit mention of
Paris providing a boost for gas):

E200 = In 2017 while President Trump was moving toward withdrawing from the Paris Agreement,
US oil majors Exxon and Chevron publicly urged him to reconsider. In a widely reported letter to
Trump, Exxon’s CEO argued that remaining in the accord would allow the US to retain “a seat
at the negotiating table to ensure a level playing field so that all energy sources and technologies
are treated equitably in an open, transparent and competitive global market.”

Thanks to the omission of details, E200 favors the strategic accommodation hypothesis. Follow-
ing the same reasoning presented in Section 5, we would have to assume that the comments
regarding “a seat at the negotiating table” under HCA are motivated by an interest in pro-
moting carbon pricing at the international level, which could benefit natural gas, even though
Exxon’s letter does not reveal that logic. But these comments seem more plausible under HSA ,
where we would understand them as an effort to hedge against less market-friendly policies
that might emerge in the international context absent US leadership at Paris—here we would
expect oil majors to support a weak but popular treaty to preclude pressure from below for
more ambitious climate policies that might spur future government action.

Notice that E2 , E20 , and E200 can all be considered members of a (vaguer) overarching class of
evidence EII = In 2017 while President Trump was moving toward withdrawing from the Paris
Agreement, US oil majors Exxon and Chevron publicly urged him to reconsider. Yet the more
specific evidentiary statements each carry distinct inferential weight—E2 very weakly favors
HCA , E20 favors HCA much more strongly, and E200 instead favors HSA —precisely because their
details differ. Two important points follow. First, as a matter of principle, small details and
nuances that we may not foresee and cannot exhaustively delineate in advance when imaging
what kinds of evidence we might encounter can matter critically for the inferential weight. In
qualitative research, analyzing likelihood ratios is therefore a task to be undertaken after data
collection, once the actual evidence is in hand (Jaynes 2003). Second, care should be taken
to report adequate detail in the evidence rather than resorting to high-level summaries, so
that readers can independently evaluate whether they agree with the author’s analysis of its
inferential import.

3. Quantifying Weights of Evidence



In Section 4, we noted that there will always be some subjectivity when quantifying weighs
of evidence in qualitative research. Readers might wonder if there are additional rules for as-
signing decibels that could curtail subjectivity, beyond the guidance we have provided. But
any effort to devise algorithmic rules for assigning inferential weight would falter due to the
unlimited diversity of qualitative information that could materialize. Small details that cannot
be adequately imagined in advance may matter significantly for inferential import (Appendix
B.2), so we must instead analyze evidence once it is hand, following the Bayesian reasoning
process we have already described: mentally inhabit the world of each hypothesis, evaluate how
much more expected evidence E seems under Hj relative to Hk , provide a clear written expla-
nation of that assessment, and then choose however many decibels best reflect one’s inferential
judgement about the weight of evidence—using Fig. 1, the corresponding sound files, and the
examples we provide as a basis for calibration.

If the analyst has done a good job of explaining their reasoning about relative likelihoods, the
basis for their choice of decibels should be clear (again, aside from some inherent arbitrariness).
But if the analyst has not done a good job of explaining their reasoning, if that reasoning
does not seem to justify the decibels assigned, or if other scholars disagree with the reasoning
expounded, they should ask for further justification or revision.

Some scholars have asked about the possibility of coarse graining weights of evidence into
discreet categories, e.g., 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30 dB, which might at first glance appear less
arbitrary, rather than retaining a continuous scale. This would not be an appropriate practice.
For scholars who prefer a more coarse-grained approach, we advocate simply using the odds-
ratio approach described in Section 2 that uses qualitative adjectives to describe inferential
weight and does not involve quantification. But excessive coarse-gaining of the logarithmic
scale (a) would defeat the purpose of making our judgements more precise—e.g., some pieces
of “moderate” evidence may merit closer to 12 dB while others might merit closer to 17 dB;
(b) would make it more difficult to reach consensus on weights of evidence—e.g., instead of
splitting the difference between one scholar’s judgment of say 5 dB and a colleague’s somewhat
higher 10 dB to obtain 7 dB, they would have to agree to settle on either 5 dB or 10 dB, with
no possibility for actual compromise; and (c) would risk accumulating substantial round-off
errors when aggregating multiple pieces of evidence—e.g., if we consider ten observations that

we would each judge to provide 3 dB in favor of H1 over H2 , but we employ a coarse-grained
scale that associates “weak” evidence with 5 dB, the reported net weight of evidence would
overstate our beliefs by a sizable 20 dB. That said, differences of less than a decibel or two are
generally imperceptible, so implicitly a natural level of course-graining already exists (e.g., we
would not assign weights of say, 5.5 dB or 10.25 dB).
